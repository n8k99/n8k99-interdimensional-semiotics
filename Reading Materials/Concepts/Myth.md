# Myth

In [[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]]'s semiology, myth is not a false story or an archaic narrative but a second-order semiological system — a structural operation that takes the complete [[Sign]] of a first-order language and repurposes it as a mere [[Signifier]] for a new, ideological [[Signified]]. The mechanism is precise: myth empties the first-order sign of its particular history and context, retaining only its form, then fills that form with a new "concept" — an ideological content that presents itself as natural, eternal, and beyond question. The wrestler's body becomes "the spectacle of suffering." The photograph of a Black soldier saluting becomes "French imperialism is universal." History is emptied out; nature is poured in.

Myth's operation presupposes and parasitizes [[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics|Saussure]]'s sign model. Where Saussure establishes that the bond between signifier and signified is arbitrary, Barthes shows how myth disguises that arbitrariness through [[Naturalization]] — making culturally contingent meanings appear inevitable. [[Foucault - Order of Things|Foucault]]'s [[Episteme]] operates at a deeper level but through an analogous mechanism: not transforming particular signs into ideological vehicles, but structuring the very conditions under which signs, knowledge, and truth become possible. [[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By|Lakoff and Johnson]]'s critique of "the myths of objectivism and subjectivism" (chapters 25-28) addresses the same territory from cognitive science: the conceptual metaphors we live by are myths in Barthes' structural sense, naturalizing one framing of reality as the only possible one.

The concept of myth thus reveals a fundamental vulnerability in all sign systems: because meaning is constructed rather than given, it is always available for capture, recoding, and ideological deployment.

## Cross-Book Development

[[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]]'s formulation in "Myth Today" — the theoretical essay that concludes *Mythologies* — draws directly on [[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics|Saussure]]'s tripartite model of signifier, signified, and sign, but displaces it from linguistics to cultural analysis. Barthes maps the Saussurean model twice: once at the first order (where language produces meaning) and once at the second order (where myth captures and recodes that meaning). The key insight is that myth is a [[Metalanguage]] — a language about language, a system that speaks about the first-order system by colonizing it. This double articulation is what gives myth its peculiar power: it can parasitize any first-order language, from photography to cooking to architecture, because its operation is structural rather than content-specific.

[[Foucault - Order of Things|Foucault]] does not use the word "myth," but his analysis of epistemic shifts performs an operation that both deepens and displaces Barthes' project. Where Barthes asks how ideology captures signs, Foucault asks what determines the very framework within which signs can function as vehicles of knowledge. The Renaissance episteme's organization by resemblance, the Classical episteme's organization by representation, and the Modern episteme's organization by finitude are not myths in Barthes' sense — they are the conditions under which myths (and anti-myths, and critiques of myth) become possible. [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions|Kuhn]]'s paradigm concept offers a parallel from the history of science: normal science operates within a paradigm that naturalizes its assumptions much as myth naturalizes ideology, and paradigm shifts rupture that naturalization much as Barthes' mythological criticism does. [[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By|Lakoff and Johnson]] bring myth into the cognitive domain by showing that the "myths of objectivism and subjectivism" are not simply philosophical positions but are themselves grounded in conceptual metaphors — the myth of objectivism relies on the metaphor KNOWLEDGE IS SEEING, the myth of subjectivism on UNDERSTANDING IS FEELING. Myth, in their hands, is not just a semiological operation but a cognitive one, rooted in the body's metaphorical structuring of abstract thought.

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

Myth is central to [[Interdimensional Semiotics]] because it demonstrates that semiotic systems are not merely carriers of meaning but active transformers of it. The mythological operation — emptying a sign of its first-order content and refilling it with ideological purpose — is a form of semiotic mutation, and IS must account for how such mutations propagate across dimensional boundaries. If a myth achieves sufficient semiotic gravitational mass (sufficient integration into a culture's network of signs), does it persist across dimensional translation even when its ideological content loses its original referent? The IS framework suggests it might: a myth's structural form — its double articulation, its capacity for [[Naturalization]] — could survive even when its specific content becomes unintelligible. This raises the possibility that myth is not just a cultural phenomenon but a structural invariant of sign systems complex enough to develop second-order operations. Any semiotic substrate capable of metalanguage would be capable of myth, making it a potential universal of [[The Noosphere]] rather than a local feature of human culture.

## In the IS Reading List

- [[Barthes - Mythologies]] — "Myth Today" and the concrete analyses preceding it: myth as second-order semiological system, analyzed across French culture from wrestling to advertising to photography
- [[Foucault - Order of Things]] — Preface and Part I: episteme as a deeper structural analog, determining the conditions of possibility for knowledge and its mythological capture
- [[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By]] — Chs. 25-28: myths of objectivism and subjectivism; conceptual metaphors that naturalize particular framings of reality
- [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions]] — Chs. 2-5: normal science as paradigm-bound activity that naturalizes its assumptions
