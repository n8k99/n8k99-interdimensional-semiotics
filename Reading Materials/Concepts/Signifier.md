# Signifier

In [[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics|Saussure]]'s structural linguistics, the signifier is the material, perceptible component of the [[Sign]] — the sound-image (*image acoustique*) that serves as the sensory vehicle for meaning. It is not the physical sound itself but the psychological impression of sound, the mental trace that allows a sign to function even in silent thought. The signifier exists only in relation to its partner, the [[Signified]], and its identity is determined not by any positive quality but by its difference from all other signifiers in the system.

[[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]] transforms the signifier's role in his analysis of [[Myth]]. At the second order of signification, the mythical signifier is not a sound-image but a complete first-order sign — already laden with meaning — that myth empties out and repurposes as mere form. The photograph of a Black soldier saluting the French flag is, at the first order, a full sign with its own history and context; myth seizes it, drains that fullness, and refills it as a signifier for "French imperialism is natural and universal." [[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By|Lakoff and Johnson]] offer an analogous but cognitively grounded version: the source domain of a conceptual metaphor functions as a kind of experiential signifier, providing the concrete, bodily form through which abstract concepts become thinkable. WAR is the signifier-domain through which we think about ARGUMENT; JOURNEY is the signifier-domain through which we think about LIFE.

## Cross-Book Development

[[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics|Saussure]]'s treatment of the signifier in the *Course* (Part I, Chapters 1-2) insists on two points that reverberate through the entire reading list. First, the signifier is arbitrary — no natural bond connects a given sound-image to its concept. Second, the signifier is linear — it unfolds in time, in sequence, unlike the signified which can be grasped simultaneously. This linearity grounds the syntagmatic axis of language, the sequential chain in which signs combine. [[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]] retains the arbitrariness principle but exploits it politically: because the signifier's connection to its signified is conventional rather than natural, myth can sever that connection and forge a new one. The operation described in "Myth Today" depends entirely on the signifier's structural availability for re-coding. What Saussure describes neutrally — the signifier has no intrinsic meaning — Barthes reveals as a vulnerability that ideology exploits systematically.

[[Foucault - Order of Things|Foucault]]'s archaeology introduces a historical dimension absent from both Saussure and Barthes. In the Renaissance episteme, the signifier was not arbitrary but was bound to its referent through resemblance — words bore physical marks of their connection to things. The Classical episteme broke this bond and established the signifier as a transparent representational instrument, its materiality ideally invisible. The Modern episteme discovers the signifier's opacity: language has its own thickness, its own history, and the signifier can no longer pretend to be a clear window onto meaning. [[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By|Lakoff and Johnson]] challenge Saussure's arbitrariness from a different angle entirely. The source domain of a conceptual metaphor is not arbitrarily linked to its target but motivated by embodied experience — we understand ARGUMENT as WAR not by cultural convention alone but because argumentation involves the same bodily schemas (attack, defense, counterattack) as physical conflict. The signifier, in this view, is grounded in the body.

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

The signifier poses a direct challenge for [[Interdimensional Semiotics]]: if meaning persists across dimensional substrates, what happens to the material form that carries it? The IS framework's commitment to substrate-independent meaning implies that the signifier is the most variable component of the sign under dimensional translation — sound-images, visual forms, and bodily gestures may have no equivalent in an alien semiotic substrate. Yet meaning, the IS hypothesis suggests, can survive the total replacement of its signifiers. This points toward a view where the relational structure among signifiers (their differential values within a system) is what persists, even as the individual signifiers themselves are replaced by functionally equivalent elements in the new substrate. The signifier is, in IS terms, the disposable body of meaning — the part that changes so that the structure can remain.

## In the IS Reading List

- [[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics]] — Part I, Chs. 1-2: sound-image as the perceptible half of the sign; arbitrariness and linearity of the signifier
- [[Barthes - Mythologies]] — "Myth Today": the first-order sign becomes the mythical signifier, emptied and repurposed for ideological signification
- [[Foucault - Order of Things]] — Ch. 2 ("The Prose of the World"): the signifier in the Renaissance as materially connected to its referent through resemblance
