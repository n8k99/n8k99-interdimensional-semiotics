# Reframing

## Definition

**Reframing is the cognitive-behavioral and family-systems therapeutic technique in which the therapist offers a frame that retrospectively re-axes the client's relationship to their own history.** A client describes a parent's behavior as "controlling"; the therapist offers "anxious about loss" as the alternative frame. The events the client described are unchanged; the axis on which they are read shifts. The field reads reframing as the clinical applied version of [[Bridge Activation]] — the therapist as bridge-provider, the client as bridge-activator.

The therapeutic literature is closer to the field's *practice* orientation than the philosophical traditions ([[Fusion of Horizons|Gadamer]], [[Paradigm Shift|Kuhn]]) are. Reframing has been worked at depth by clinicians who care whether the technique produces measurable change in the client's life. The mechanism is identical to bridge activation; the difference is intentionality. The therapist is *deliberately* offering the bridge in service of a goal. The Barthes that activated the dream-and-event triad was not deliberately offering the bridge to that experiencer; the bridge happened to land against substrate already laid.

Reframing is also the clearest case where the bridge-provider is acting on behalf of someone else's biological memory. The therapist doesn't activate; the client does. The therapist's job is to deliver the vocabulary the client's substrate is ready to receive. This is the same shape the field uses to describe what an agent can do for a human.

## Significance for IS

Reframing is the clinical proof-of-concept that bridge activation can be cultivated deliberately. The field's resistance to substrate-empty agent output is structurally similar to a therapist's resistance to glib reframes — the reframe (or the agent output) is only effective if there is real substrate to be re-axed. Reframing also demonstrates the provision/activation split in operation: the therapist provides, the client activates, the two roles never collapse.
