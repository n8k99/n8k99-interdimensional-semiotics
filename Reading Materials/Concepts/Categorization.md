# Categorization

Categorization — the fundamental cognitive process of grouping entities into kinds — is the bedrock operation of thought and the central subject of [[Lakoff - Women Fire and Dangerous Things|Lakoff's]] *Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things*. Part I of that work mounts a sustained argument that categorization is not based on classical necessary-and-sufficient conditions but on cognitive models, family resemblance, prototypes, and embodied experience. Categories have graded structure, fuzzy boundaries, and central members; they are organized by ICMs, not by logical definitions. This is not a minor technical revision but a wholesale rejection of the objectivist epistemology that has dominated Western thought since Aristotle.

The concept resonates powerfully across the reading list. [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions|Kuhn's]] paradigm concept includes the idea that scientific exemplars teach practitioners to group phenomena into "similarity sets" (Postscript, line 8206) — categorization as a learned skill acquired through exposure to solved problems, not through the application of explicit rules. [[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]] demonstrates that cultural categories (what counts as "French," what counts as "natural") are constructed through mythological sign-systems that naturalize contingent groupings. [[Hofstadter - Godel Escher Bach|Hofstadter]] treats categorization as pattern recognition: the ability to perceive structural correspondence between superficially different entities, which is the basis of analogy and, by extension, of all higher cognition.

Categorization thus sits at the intersection of epistemology, cognitive science, and semiotics. How a system carves the world into kinds determines what it can think, what it can say, and what it can know.

## Cross-Book Development

[[Lakoff - Women Fire and Dangerous Things|Lakoff's]] account in *Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things* (Part I, ch. 2-6) replaces the classical theory of categories with a cognitive theory grounded in four types of evidence: prototype effects (some members of a category are more representative than others), basic-level effects (there is a privileged level of categorization corresponding to embodied interaction), radial structure (categories extend from a central case through conventionalized links, ch. 6), and metonymic models (a subcategory or member can stand for the category as a whole, pp. 77-79, 107-8). Behind all of these stands the ICM (ch. 4): the structured cognitive model that organizes a domain and produces prototype effects when it fails to map perfectly onto reality.

[[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions|Kuhn's]] contribution is to show that categorization is not just a cognitive process but a social and historical one. Scientists within a paradigm learn to categorize phenomena through shared exemplars — they learn to see certain problems as instances of the same type, and this learned similarity perception constitutes the paradigm's practical content. When a paradigm shifts, the similarity sets change: phenomena that were grouped together are now separated, and previously unrelated phenomena are now seen as instances of the same kind. This is why Kuhn describes paradigm shifts as changes in "world" rather than changes in interpretation — the categories through which the world is perceived have reorganized.

[[Kurzweil - How to Create a Mind|Kurzweil's]] Pattern Recognition Theory of Mind provides a mechanism: the neocortex's hierarchical modules learn categories by building pattern recognizers at successively higher levels of abstraction. A low-level module recognizes an edge; higher modules recognize a letter, a word, a concept. Categorization, on this account, is literally what the brain does — the progressive grouping of input patterns into equivalence classes. Clark and Chalmers' extended cognition thesis adds that these categorization processes can extend beyond the brain into external tools: a field guide, a classification system, a database — any structure that supports the grouping of entities into kinds.

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

Categorization is where [[Interdimensional Semiotics]] meets its deepest epistemological challenge. If categories are not discovered in nature but constructed by cognitive systems through embodied interaction, ICMs, and cultural convention, then there is no guarantee that different dimensions will carve the world at the same joints. The IS question is not whether entities exist across dimensions — it is whether the category structures through which those entities are recognized and organized are commensurable.

This connects to the IS concept of semiotic gravitational mass: a category with deep grounding in embodied experience (basic-level categories tied to motor programs and gestalt perception) may have greater mass and persist more readily across substrates than abstract categories defined by propositional ICMs. It also connects to [[The Noosphere]] as a space where category systems from different cognitive architectures meet, merge, and potentially become mutually unintelligible — a kind of interdimensional Kuhnian incommensurability operating not between scientific paradigms but between embodied meaning-worlds.
