# Disclosure Shear

Disclosure Shear is the semiotic stress created when an act of disclosure changes different audiences' honoring conditions at different rates, so that one communication meant to stabilize a Tinkerbell object instead pulls its interpretive basin out of alignment.

The concept constrains [[Basin Maintenance]] and [[Symbolic Persistence Theory]]. Some signs survive by being clarified; others survive because their supporting audiences are not forced to process every substrate condition at once. Disclosure Shear appears when transparency is necessary for trust but dangerous to the very coordination that trust requires.

It is not the same as secrecy. Secrecy withholds. Disclosure Shear names the deformation caused by saying. A risk statement, ethics page, correction notice, incident report, amended constitution, or public provenance note may create useful [[Common Knowledge]] for one audience while creating panic, opportunism, denial, or interpretive overreach in another. The disclosure is true, and the truth still moves the basin.

The canonical distributed-systems analogue is the [[Coordinated Attack Problem]]. No finite message guarantees perfect coordinated action across an unreliable channel; each acknowledgment requires another acknowledgment. Disclosure in a Tinkerbell system has the same regress with a harder edge: every message sent to produce shared knowledge becomes new substrate for interpretation, reliance, and future denial. The disclosure does not merely report the state. It becomes part of the state.

Disclosure Shear is measured by the divergence between intended stabilizing effect and actual audience-specific drift:

- Low shear: the disclosure makes the relevant fact more jointly actionable without materially destabilizing honoring.
- Medium shear: the disclosure stabilizes one audience while perturbing another.
- High shear: the disclosure becomes the dominant event, displacing the condition it was meant to explain.

The ethical implication is not "disclose less." The legal implication is not "disclose everything." The IntSem implication is narrower and more severe: any communication that changes shared memory must be analyzed as an intervention in the basin, not as a neutral transfer of facts.
