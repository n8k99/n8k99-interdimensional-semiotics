# Natural Interpretation

In [[Feyerabend - Against Method|Feyerabend]]'s epistemology, a natural interpretation is a pre-theoretical idea so closely fused with sensory observation that it appears to be a fact of nature, when it is actually an implicit theoretical commitment that can be identified and replaced. Developed across chapters 6-7 of *Against Method*, the concept reveals that what we call "observation" is never theory-neutral: every act of perception is shaped by natural interpretations that function as invisible lenses, structuring experience before conscious thought begins. The term "natural" is precisely the problem — these interpretations feel natural only because they are so deeply embedded that the subject cannot distinguish them from raw sensory data.

Feyerabend's central illustration is Galileo's defense of Copernicanism. The Aristotelian argument against terrestrial motion — that objects dropped from a tower fall straight down rather than being left behind — seemed like an irrefutable observation. Feyerabend shows that it was not an observation at all but a natural interpretation: the Aristotelian framework had fused the concept of "absolute motion" with the act of seeing, so that the straight fall of a stone appeared to be an immediate perceptual datum rather than a theory-laden inference. Galileo's genius was not in making better observations but in identifying and replacing this natural interpretation with another (relative motion and circular inertia), thereby transforming the same sensory experience into evidence for rather than against Copernicanism.

The concept connects directly to [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions|Kuhn]]'s theory-ladenness of observation and to [[Lakoff - Women Fire and Dangerous Things|Lakoff]]'s idealized cognitive models (ICMs). All three accounts converge on the claim that perception is not a passive reception of data but an active, framework-dependent construction — though they differ on whether the relevant framework is a scientific paradigm (Kuhn), a set of philosophical assumptions (Feyerabend), or a cognitive architecture (Lakoff).

## Cross-Book Development

Feyerabend develops the concept of natural interpretation across chapters 6-7 of *Against Method* as part of his extended analysis of Galileo's argumentative strategy. Chapter 6 ("The Tower Argument") demonstrates that the classical argument against terrestrial motion rested on an unexamined natural interpretation — the identification of perceived motion with absolute motion. Chapter 7 shows how Galileo replaced this natural interpretation with another: the principle of relativity, which rendered the same observation (straight vertical fall) compatible with a moving Earth. The replacement was not a matter of logic or evidence but of what Feyerabend calls "propaganda and psychological tricks" — Galileo had to change how his readers saw before he could change what they believed.

The concept has a direct parallel in [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions|Kuhn]]'s account of paradigm-dependent perception. Kuhn argues (particularly in Chapter X, "Revolutions as Changes of World View") that scientists working under different paradigms literally see different things when looking at the same phenomena. Where Feyerabend locates the mechanism in philosophical assumptions fused with sensation, Kuhn locates it in the paradigm's exemplars — the concrete problem-solutions that train scientists to see certain patterns and ignore others. The convergence is significant: both argue that observation is constituted, not merely influenced, by theoretical commitments, and that these commitments are typically invisible to the observer.

Lakoff's *Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things* provides the cognitive-scientific mechanism for what Kuhn and Feyerabend describe at the level of intellectual history. Lakoff's ICMs — idealized cognitive models that structure categorization at a pre-conscious level — are the cognitive equivalent of natural interpretations. They are not theories that the mind applies to experience but structures built into the cognitive apparatus that determine what counts as experience in the first place. The connection to Barthes's concept of naturalization (the process by which cultural conventions come to seem natural and self-evident) extends the chain further: natural interpretations, ICMs, and naturalization are all instances of the same phenomenon — the invisibility of the interpretive framework to those who inhabit it.

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

Natural interpretation is one of the most consequential concepts for [[Interdimensional Semiotics]] because it identifies the deepest layer of semiotic commitment — the interpretive substrate that is so fundamental that it registers as unmediated experience rather than as interpretation. The IS challenge of symbolic persistence across dimensions is not primarily a challenge of translating explicit signs (words, symbols, formal structures) but of identifying and mediating the natural interpretations that each dimension's inhabitants cannot distinguish from raw reality. A sign that successfully crosses a dimensional boundary might still be misinterpreted if the receiving dimension's natural interpretations transform it before conscious interpretation begins.

This connects to [[AnthropomorphicFrameworks]] and the architecture of [[The Noosphere]]. Human natural interpretations — the implicit assumptions about space, time, causality, and identity that structure human perception — constitute a specific semiotic substrate. The IS framework must determine whether natural interpretations are artifacts of particular cognitive architectures (and thus variable across dimensions) or whether they point to invariant features of any semiotic system capable of supporting consciousness. The answer determines whether interdimensional communication requires merely a translation protocol or a wholesale reconstruction of the perceptual apparatus — the kind of interpretive revolution that Galileo accomplished, but scaled to dimensions rather than theories.
