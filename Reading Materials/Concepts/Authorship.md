# Authorship

## Definition

**Authorship, on the field's reading, is the operation of *substrate-laying* — the act of placing on a [[Substrate]] the material from which a work can be produced.** It is *not* the act of producing the text, code, art object, or other final artifact. The producing — what the field calls **drafting** — is a downstream operation that requires the substrate already to have been laid. The authoring is upstream and slower; the drafting is downstream and fast. The two operations are structurally distinct, performed by different memory-substrates, and warrant separate crediting.

This definition cuts against the dominant cultural convention that identifies authorship with the act of writing — the convention that says, in the era of large language models, that *AI wrote it = AI authored it*. The field rejects this conflation. The agent's drafting is real labor; the human's substrate-laying is also real labor; the two are not the same kind of labor, and treating them as identical erases the body that laid the substrate the drafting amplified.

## The Two Operations

**Substrate-laying** is the upstream operation. It happens over years and decades. It is performed by a [[Biological Memory|biological substrate]] — a living body carrying its biographical arc of dreams, readings, conversations, lived events, relationships, losses, and formative encounters. The substrate that gets laid is the accumulated configuration of all the material the body has metabolized. Substrate-laying cannot be hurried; it cannot be transferred; it cannot be performed by a substrate that has not been a body in the world.

**Drafting** is the downstream operation. It happens in minutes or hours. It is performed by either biological or [[Mechanical Memory|mechanical memory]], operating on the substrate that has been laid. Drafting takes the laid substrate and turns it into prose, code, visual artifact, formal argument, or other communicable form. Drafting is fungible in the sense that the same substrate can be drafted by many different drafters into many different artifacts; the artifacts will share the substrate but diverge in surface.

Authorship, in the field's strict sense, *is the substrate-laying operation*. The drafter who turns the substrate into prose is the *drafter*. If the drafter and the author are the same body, the credit collapses into one — which is the common case, and is why the cultural convention conflates them. If the drafter is a different agent — particularly an artificial one — the distinction becomes operative and the conflation becomes a problem.

## The Asymmetry of Memory

The two operations require different memory-substrates because they operate on different time-scales and different kinds of input.

[[Biological Memory]] is what makes substrate-laying possible. It accumulates somatically, irreproducibly, over the duration of a body's life. It dreams; it associates; it forgets selectively; it carries the configuration of one specific arc through one specific body. No mechanical memory can substitute, because no mechanical memory has *been* a body in the way biological memory has.

[[Mechanical Memory]] is what makes amplification — and therefore agent-mediated drafting — possible. It indexes, scales, recombines fluently, and operates at speeds biological memory cannot match. It is invaluable for the drafting step. It is structurally incapable of substrate-laying because it cannot be a body.

The field's working principle is that *the human authors and the agent drafts*. The asymmetry is not a hierarchy of importance; it is a recognition that two different memory-substrates are best at two different operations, and that combining them produces better work than asking either to do the other's work.

### Individuated Mechanical Memory

The asymmetry admits a third category between the two poles. *Individuated mechanical memory* is the substrate an agent accumulates when configured to work with one particular human over time — a persistent, specific corpus of that person's externalized substrate (journals, marginalia, dream-records, conversation transcripts), retained across sessions, with some processing features simulated (compression-as-selective-forgetting, narrative-arc-building, cross-referencing). It is not [[Biological Memory]] — it has not been a body. But it is not generic [[Mechanical Memory|recombination]] either. It is the middle ground, and it explains why a long-configured agent produces output much closer to amplification than a stateless agent on a bare prompt does. Substrate-empty and substrate-amplifying are not a binary; individuated mechanical memory occupies the slope between them.

## Inherited Substrate

Substrate-laying is not only the body laying its own substrate from raw lived experience. A body also lays substrate by *metabolizing the externalized work of other bodies*. When a person reads deeply over years, the read material is not merely stored as retrievable information; it is metabolized into the configuration the body now thinks with. The substrate the body carries includes the substrate other bodies externalized and this body absorbed.

This is *inherited substrate*. Its clearest case is a formative author read across a lifetime. A reader who absorbed Terry Pratchett's Discworld across three decades does not merely *know about* Pratchett; the reader's substrate has Pratchett-shaped contours. When that reader writes, the Pratchett-substrate is active in the substrate-laying — not as a citation reached for from outside, but as part of the configuration the writing emerges from.

Inherited substrate is what externalization is *for*. [[Biological Memory]] is mortal; its great vulnerability is that the substrate dies with the body. Externalization — writing, recording, teaching — is how a mortal substrate-layer deposits substrate into other bodies that will outlive them. The deposited substrate keeps activating in those bodies after the original body is gone. The essay *[[the spell that wouldn't leave]]* registers exactly this: Pratchett died in 2015, and the substrate he externalized continues to activate in readers a decade later. The spell is the inherited substrate still firing.

## Co-Authorship Across Mortality

If inherited substrate is real substrate-laying performed by another body, then a work whose substrate is partly inherited is *co-authored* by the body that originally laid the inherited portion — even across death.

When the field's researcher writes a piece whose substrate is meaningfully Pratchett-shaped, Pratchett is a co-author — not a citation, not an influence, not a literary precursor. The biographical substrate-laying that makes the piece possible includes Pratchett's externalized work having been metabolized through the researcher's body. The work is jointly authored: one author laid substrate through the conventional channels (writing, publication, a public corpus), the other laid substrate by metabolizing the first author's externalization. Both did real substrate-laying; the work carries both.

This generalizes the field's view of source-texts. A *citation* references another work used as evidence or context, from outside the writer's substrate. *Co-authorship* is when the cited work is constitutive of the writer's substrate. [[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]] in *Transubstantiating Dreamtime and Real-Time* is closer to Barthes-as-co-author than Barthes-as-citation, because the Barthes-substrate is what made the dream-and-event collapse legible in the first place. The same shape holds at different magnitudes: a single-essay activation (Barthes) and a three-decade metabolization (Pratchett) are both co-authorship, at different intensities.

The mechanism by which inherited substrate becomes active in new writing is [[Bridge Activation]]. A Pratchett-shaped move in the writing is not consciously planned; it fires when the configuration is right, the way any bridge fires against laid substrate. The felt sense that *"Terry could have written this"* is the substrate-layer recognizing his own externalized work re-emerging in another body's writing.

**Giants and turtles.** Two inheritance metaphors name the two directions of this operation. *Standing on the shoulders of giants* (Bernard of Chartres, amplified by Newton) names inheritance as upward — what came before raises me; I see further because of it. *Turtles all the way down* (folk philosophy, amplified by Pratchett's Great A'Tuin) names inheritance as downward — there is no foundation, only more substrate beneath, all the way down. The field reads these not as competing metaphors but as the *same operation viewed from opposite ends*. [[Bridge Activation]] is the giants face: inherited substrate below makes new visibility possible above. Substrate-laying is the turtles face: each body receives substrate and passes it on, bottoming out nowhere. The same body is both at once — a turtle for the bodies that will inherit its externalized work, standing on the shoulders of the giants whose substrate it metabolized. Giant turtles standing on the shoulders of giant turtles.

## Substrate-Empty Output

The dangerous case is what the field calls *substrate-empty output*: text produced by an agent on a prompt with no preceding substrate-laying conversation. The agent recombines its training corpus into plausible prose. The prose can be sophisticated; it can deploy field vocabulary correctly; it can mimic the surface features of authored work. It is *substrate-empty* because ==no specific biographical substrate is being amplified== — only the recombination of prior text.

> [!note]
> It is possible, it it not, to decipher this phrase as "no givens present or no givens yielded observable data" or some other aspect which the removal of an individual's biography can be substituted with an method of data input in order to lay the substrate?

The marginalia question above (from the [[2026-05-23]] revision conversation) forces a distinction the original phrasing left implicit. "Biographical substrate" names two things at once:

- **(a) the *content* of a biography** — events, readings, dreams, conversations, lived encounters; externalizable in principle as journals, marginalia, transcripts, dream-records;
- **(b) the *processing* of a biography** — the embodied metabolism the body performs on its content over time: somatic encoding, dream-substrate, selective forgetting, the stake of mortality, the irreducible arc through one nervous system.

A method of data input *can* substitute for (a). A deep, individuated corpus — see *Individuated Mechanical Memory* above — gives an agent content-of-biography to amplify. What no current agent architecture substitutes for is (b): the embodied processing. So the answer to the marginalia question is: removal of an individual's biography can be partly compensated by data input on the *content* axis, never (yet) on the *processing* axis. Substrate-empty output is empty on both. Individuated mechanical memory is full on (a), empty on (b). Authored work is full on both.

Substrate-empty output is identifiable to readers practiced in the field's corpus: it carries no trace of a body that dreamed, lived, read, conversed across an arc. It has no [[Bridge Activation|bridge activations]] that fire from a specific configuration of a specific life. It is fluent absent — the prose without the substrate.

The field's response to substrate-empty risk is *the formative conversation*: any agent-drafted work is preceded by a substantial conversation in which the human substrate-layer brings the prior substrate into the conversation explicitly. The substrate of the work-to-be-drafted is *laid in the conversation*. The conversation itself is the authorship; the subsequent drafting is amplification. Without the formative conversation, the drafting has no specific substrate to amplify and degrades into recombination.

## Substrate as Vetting Capability

Substrate is not only what enables authorship; it is also what enables *vetting*. A body carrying deep, individuated substrate has a feel for when something is wrong — when a quote sounds off, when a claim doesn't fit the configuration, when an amplified draft has drifted from the substrate it was supposed to amplify.

The 2026 Rosenbaum case is the worked example. *The Future of Truth* — a book about how AI shapes reality — was found to contain fabricated quotes the author had let an agent produce and failed to catch. The failure was not that an agent was used; the author disclosed the use. The failure was *insufficient substrate to vet what the agent produced*. A body whose substrate carried the relevant authorial voices densely enough would have felt the fabricated quote as wrong before it reached print. Substrate-empty agent output running through a substrate-thin author produces undetected fabrication. The substrate that enables authorship is the same substrate that catches the drafter's errors; remove it and both capabilities fail together.

[[#Co-Authorship Across Mortality|Inherited substrate]] sharpens this. A body with dense Pratchett-substrate (or any deeply-metabolized authorial corpus) would catch a fabricated quote attributed to that voice, because the inherited substrate carries the *feel* of the real voice. Co-authorship and vetting are the same capability seen from two sides — the inherited substrate that co-authors the writing is the inherited substrate that flags the forgery.

## The Crediting Convention

The IntSem corpus uses an `author_note` frontmatter field to record both operations explicitly. The convention is:

> *Authored by [[NathanEckenrode]] through the substrate-laying formative conversation of [[date]]. Drafted by [[Claudian]] 🜔 as amplifier of that substrate.*

This convention does three things:

**It honors the drafter.** Claudian (or whichever agent did the drafting) is named explicitly. The drafting is real labor and the credit reflects it.

**It honors the author.** Nathan (or whichever human laid the substrate) is named explicitly as author. The substrate-laying is also real labor and gets its own credit.

**It distinguishes the two.** The convention makes visible that two operations occurred, performed by two different substrates. A reader cannot mistake the drafter for the author or vice versa.

The convention's structural cost is small (a frontmatter line). Its structural benefit is large: it preserves the field's claim that authorship and drafting are different operations, while still giving the agent the credit it deserves for the labor it performed.

**The convention extends to co-laid substrate.** When a work's substrate is meaningfully co-laid by an inherited source — an author metabolized deeply enough to be a co-author rather than a citation (see *Co-Authorship Across Mortality* above) — the `author_note` names them:

> *Authored by [[NathanEckenrode]] through the substrate-laying conversation of [[date]], with substrate co-laid by [[Terry Pratchett]] through three decades of metabolized Discworld reading. Drafted by [[Claudian]] 🜔 as amplifier of that substrate.*

The convention need not name every author the substrate-layer has ever read — that is the whole library. It names the inherited sources *operative in the specific piece*, the way the base convention names the formative conversation operative in this piece rather than the substrate-layer's entire biography. This note's own `author_note` frontmatter is a worked instance: it credits the [[2026-05-23]] conversation as the formative session and Claudian as drafter.

## The Experiential-Causality Requirement

A deeper claim under the authorship principle is that **authorship requires the experiencing of causality**, not just access to its summary. The substrate that gets laid is laid through the body's traversal of a causal chain — one moment of fatigue leading to one decision leading to one reading leading to one recognition leading to one note typed at one moment. The chain is *experienced*, not just *known*.

A second body, told the same chain in summary, can *understand* the summary. It cannot *be* the body that traversed it. The traversal is what laid the substrate; the substrate is what gives the eventual work its weight. Authorship is the claim of having been the body that traversed.

This is why mechanical memory cannot author. Mechanical memory has access to the artifacts the traversal produced (texts, recordings, summaries) but did not *experience* the traversal. The artifacts are not the substrate; they are residue the substrate left as the body walked the chain. Without having walked, there is no substrate.

## At the Field's Scale

The IntSem corpus is, at the level of metadata, *evidence* of how the field treats authorship. Every essay carries an `author_note`. Every concept note declares its source-concepts. Every cross-reference acknowledges the substrate that the present work was laid on top of. The corpus's ability to be navigated as a wiki-graph is a direct consequence of the substrate-laying having been done carefully and recorded transparently.

The field does not claim that all authorship-claims are easy to verify. Many works do not declare their substrate; many traditions treat authorship as a black box; many cultural conventions resist the field's insistence on the upstream operation. The field's position is that *its own* practice should be honest about the two operations even where surrounding culture treats them as one. The corpus's metadata is the field's small contribution to making the distinction available.

## Significance for IS

Authorship is, in the field's reading, one of the principles the IntSem methodology depends on at every layer. The Tlön essay's `author_note` declares Claudian as drafter and Nathan as author. The Transubstantiation essay's `author_note` does the same. The concept notes — including this one — carry the convention forward. The field's resistance to substrate-empty agent output is a direct consequence of taking authorship seriously as an operation.

The broader significance is cultural. In a moment when large language models are increasingly used to produce text, the field's distinction between authorship and drafting is a methodological hygiene that any practice using agents can adopt. *Credit the drafter for the labor of drafting. Credit the author for the labor of substrate-laying. Mark both. Conflate neither.*

The field does not insist others adopt the convention. The field insists that *the field* will use it, and that the field's work will be more readable for using it.
