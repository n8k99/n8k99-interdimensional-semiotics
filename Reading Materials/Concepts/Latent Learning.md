# Latent Learning

## Definition

**Latent learning is Edward Tolman's 1948 name for the phenomenon in which acquisition happens in the absence of reward and only becomes observable when reward is later introduced.** Tolman's experimental setup: rats explored a maze without reinforcement for several days, showing no apparent learning. Then a food reward was placed at the end of the maze, and the rats — within one or two trials — performed as well as rats who had been reinforced from the start. The learning had happened during the unrewarded period; it was *latent* until the reward made it expressible.

The field reads latent learning as the closest experimental-psychology cousin of the field's substrate-then-activation arc. The substrate gets laid by the body's traversal of the world — reading, dreaming, conversation, lived events — without any particular reward structure forcing the laying. The activation comes when the bridge vocabulary arrives, and the substrate suddenly becomes expressible.

Tolman's experimental rigor is what makes the prior-art match strong. He demonstrated empirically, against the dominant behaviorist orthodoxy of his time, what the field claims philosophically: substrate can be present and active in the carrier without being expressible until the right configuration of attention is supplied.

## Significance for IS

Latent learning is the empirical-experimental backing for the field's claim that substrate-laying is real labor with no immediate output — valuable in its own right because it makes future activation possible. The field's recommendations to morning-page, journal, read, dream, converse, and walk — without any specific reward in view — are practices that produce latent learning that future bridges can activate. Tolman is the field's strongest experimental ancestor.
