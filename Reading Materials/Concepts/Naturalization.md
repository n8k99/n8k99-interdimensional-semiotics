# Naturalization

In [[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]]'s analysis, naturalization is [[Myth]]'s central operation: the process by which historically and culturally contingent meanings are made to appear natural, inevitable, and eternal. Myth takes what is produced by history — class relations, imperial power, bourgeois values — and presents it as if it were simply the way things are. The mechanism depends on the structural insight that [[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics|Saussure]] established: the bond between [[Signifier]] and [[Signified]] is arbitrary. Because there is no natural necessity connecting form to meaning, the connection can be fabricated and then disguised as natural. Naturalization is the name for that disguise.

[[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By|Lakoff and Johnson]] demonstrate a parallel mechanism operating at the cognitive level. Conceptual metaphors naturalize abstract thought through embodied experience — the metaphor TIME IS MONEY makes a particular cultural framing of temporality (as a quantifiable, expendable resource) feel like an objective description of reality rather than a historically specific construction. The naturalization here is not ideological in Barthes' sense but cognitive: the metaphor is so deeply integrated into bodily experience that it becomes invisible, indistinguishable from direct perception. [[Foucault - Order of Things|Foucault]] provides a deeper layer of analysis by showing that naturalization operates not just at the level of individual signs or metaphors but at the level of entire knowledge systems. Each [[Episteme]] naturalizes its own organizing principles — the Renaissance naturalizes resemblance, the Classical age naturalizes representation, Modernity naturalizes the analytic categories of life, labor, and language — so completely that the organizing principle itself becomes invisible, thinkable only from outside the episteme.

## Cross-Book Development

[[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics|Saussure]]'s principle of the arbitrary sign, articulated in Part I of the *Course*, is the theoretical condition of possibility for naturalization. If the sign were naturally motivated — if sound-images bore an intrinsic connection to their concepts — then there would be nothing to naturalize; meaning would already be natural. It is precisely because the sign is arbitrary that the appearance of naturalness must be manufactured. [[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]] builds directly on this foundation throughout *Mythologies*. In the concrete analyses (the essays on wrestling, soap powders, the new Citroen, steak and chips), he demonstrates naturalization in action across domains of French culture. In "Myth Today," he formalizes the mechanism: myth transforms history into nature by moving from the first order of signification (where meaning is openly conventional) to the second order (where meaning presents itself as given). The essay on "The Great Family of Man" is perhaps his clearest demonstration: the photographic exhibition naturalizes bourgeois universalism by presenting culturally specific family structures as eternal human truths.

[[Foucault - Order of Things|Foucault]] does not use the term "naturalization," but his archaeology of knowledge performs an analogous critique at a more fundamental level. Where Barthes reveals how particular ideological contents are naturalized through myth, Foucault reveals how entire frameworks of knowledge are naturalized through epistemic embedding. The Classical age's project of taxonomic representation — its conviction that the world could be exhaustively ordered in tables and classifications — was not experienced as one possible approach to knowledge but as knowledge itself. [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions|Kuhn]]'s account of normal science describes a similar process in the history of science: the paradigm naturalizes its own assumptions so thoroughly that anomalies are invisible until they accumulate to the point of crisis. [[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By|Lakoff and Johnson]] complicate the picture by suggesting that some naturalization may not be ideological distortion but cognitive necessity: the metaphors that structure our understanding of abstract concepts are not arbitrary cultural impositions but emerge from the body's interaction with its environment. The naturalization of TIME IS MONEY is culturally specific, but the naturalization of MORE IS UP may be grounded in universal bodily experience. This raises the question of whether all naturalization is mystification or whether some forms represent genuine cognitive adaptation.

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

Naturalization poses a fundamental methodological challenge for [[Interdimensional Semiotics]]. If the purpose of IS is to study semiotic structures as they persist across dimensional boundaries, then it must be able to distinguish between what is structurally invariant and what has merely been naturalized — made to appear invariant through ideological or cognitive processes. An IS researcher encountering a semiotic system in a new dimension must ask: does this pattern persist because it reflects a genuine structural invariant of sign systems, or because it has been so thoroughly naturalized within its local substrate that it appears inevitable? The Barthesian critique of naturalization becomes, in IS terms, a diagnostic tool for identifying pseudo-universals. At the same time, the Lakoff-Johnson insight that some naturalization is cognitively grounded raises the possibility that certain [[AnthropomorphicFrameworks]] — embodied metaphors shared across species or substrates that interact with similar physical environments — might represent genuine semiotic universals rather than local naturalizations. The distinction between ideological naturalization and cognitive grounding may prove to be one of IS's most important analytical tasks.

- [[Foucault - Order of Things]] — Preface and Chs. 3-6: epistemic naturalization — how each age's organizing principle becomes invisible to those operating within it
- [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions]] — Chs. 2-4: normal science as the naturalization of paradigmatic assumptions
