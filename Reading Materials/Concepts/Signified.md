# Signified

In [[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics|Saussure]]'s structural linguistics, the signified is the concept or mental content that constitutes the invisible half of the [[Sign]]. It is not a thing in the world but a psychological entity — a node in a network of conceptual relations. Like the [[Signifier]], the signified has no positive content of its own; it is defined purely by its difference from other signifieds within the system of [[Langue]]. The signified "dog" is what it is because it is not "cat," not "wolf," not "pet" — its identity is relational through and through.

[[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]] reveals the signified's ideological dimension in his analysis of [[Myth]]. At the second order of signification, the mythical signified is not a linguistic concept but an ideological one — what Barthes calls simply "concept." It is the intentional content that myth injects into its hollowed-out signifier: "French imperialism," "Italian-ness," "the eternal nature of bourgeois values." Unlike the first-order signified, which is part of a relatively stable linguistic code, the mythical signified is historically motivated and politically interested. [[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By|Lakoff and Johnson]] reconceive the signified as the target domain of conceptual metaphor — the abstract conceptual territory (TIME, LOVE, ARGUMENT) that can only be understood through systematic projection from a concrete source domain. The target domain is, in effect, a signified that cannot stand on its own but requires the embodied scaffolding of metaphorical structure.

## Cross-Book Development

[[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics|Saussure]]'s account of the signified in the *Course* (Part I, Chapter 1) is deliberately austere. The signified is a mental impression, not a referent — linguistics studies the relationship between sound-image and concept, not between language and the external world. This move insulates the sign from questions of truth and reference, establishing semiotics as a formal science of internal relations. [[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]], writing in "Myth Today," breaks open that formal closure. The mythical signified is explicitly connected to historical and political interests; it is not a neutral concept but an ideological intention. Where Saussure's signified "tree" is defined by its place in a linguistic grid, Barthes' mythical signified "French imperialism" is defined by its function in a system of class power. The signified, in Barthes' hands, becomes the site where politics enters the sign.

[[Foucault - Order of Things|Foucault]] complicates the signified further by showing that what counts as a legitimate concept — what can occupy the position of the signified — changes across epistemes. In the Renaissance, the signified was woven into a tissue of resemblances that connected words, things, and cosmic correspondences. In the Classical age (chapters 3-4 of *The Order of Things*), the signified was purified into a representational content, a clear and distinct idea that the sign made present to the mind. The Modern episteme discovers that the signified is not a transparent idea but a product of historically specific systems of knowledge. [[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By|Lakoff and Johnson]] add the cognitive dimension: the target domain (the abstract signified of metaphor) is not an independent concept but one that is constitutively structured by its source domain. We do not first have a concept of TIME and then metaphorize it as MONEY; the metaphor TIME IS MONEY partially constitutes what time means to us. The signified, in this framework, is never fully separable from the concrete forms through which it is understood.

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

For [[Interdimensional Semiotics]], the signified is where the question of meaning's persistence becomes most acute. If the signifier is the disposable material substrate, the signified is the conceptual content that IS theory suggests should survive dimensional translation. But the reading list complicates any simple version of this claim. Saussure shows that signifieds are not self-standing concepts but positions in a differential system — meaning that what persists is not individual concepts but relational structures. Foucault shows that even the relational structure changes across epistemes, raising the question of whether dimensional translation is more like epistemic rupture than simple transcription. The IS concept of semiotic gravitational mass may help here: the signified's "mass" — its resistance to drift and distortion — is a function not of its intrinsic content but of the density of its relational connections within a sign system. A heavily connected signified (one with rich differential relations to many other concepts) would persist more robustly across substrates than an isolated one. This connects the signified to the broader IS interest in [[connectomes]] and [[The Noosphere]] as networks of conceptual persistence.
