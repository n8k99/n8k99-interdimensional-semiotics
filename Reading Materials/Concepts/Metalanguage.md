# Metalanguage

In the formal linguistic sense, a metalanguage is a language used to describe, analyze, or make statements about another language (the "object-language" or "language-object"). [[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]] appropriates this concept for his semiology of [[Myth]], arguing that myth is a metalanguage — a second-order semiological system that speaks about and parasitizes a first-order language. Myth does not create meaning from scratch but takes existing signs and re-signifies them at a higher order. The linguistic sign "tree" becomes, in myth, a vehicle for "Frenchness" or "nature" or "tradition." The first-order meaning is not destroyed but hollowed out, thinned to a mere form, made available as raw material for ideological recoding.

The concept bridges [[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics|Saussure]]'s structural linguistics (where [[Langue]] constitutes the first-order code that any metalanguage must presuppose) and [[Foucault - Order of Things|Foucault]]'s archaeology of knowledge (where [[Discourse]] functions as a system that determines what can be said about other systems of knowledge). In [[Hofstadter - Godel Escher Bach|Hofstadter]]'s framework, metalanguage connects to the levels problem in formal systems: a system that can make statements about itself is operating at a meta-level, and this capacity for self-reference is what generates both Godel's incompleteness theorems and the strange loops that Hofstadter identifies with consciousness.

Metalanguage thus marks the point where a semiotic system becomes capable of reflecting on itself — and where that reflection opens the door to both critical analysis and ideological capture.

## Cross-Book Development

[[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]]'s formulation in "Myth Today" establishes the most developed semiological account of metalanguage. He draws the schema explicitly: language (the first-order system, with its own signifier, signified, and sign) becomes the language-object upon which myth constructs its second-order system. The crucial point is that this metalinguistic operation is not analytical but parasitic — myth does not study the first-order language but feeds on it, using its completed signs as raw material. This distinguishes mythical metalanguage from scientific metalanguage: where the linguist's metalanguage aims to describe the object-language transparently, myth's metalanguage aims to transform it ideologically. Barthes is explicit that semiology itself — his own critical practice — is a third-order metalanguage, a language that speaks about myth's language about language.

[[Foucault - Order of Things|Foucault]]'s archaeology operates at a different level of metalinguistic analysis. In *The Order of Things* (chapter 10, "The Human Sciences"), Foucault shows how the human sciences (psychology, sociology, literary analysis) function as metalanguages that take the fundamental domains of life, labor, and language as their objects. But unlike Barthes' mythical metalanguage, Foucault's discursive metalanguage does not merely re-signify its object — it constitutes it. The discourse of biology does not parasitize a pre-existing concept of "life" but produces what "life" means within the Modern episteme. [[Hofstadter - Godel Escher Bach|Hofstadter]] pushes metalanguage into formal and cognitive territory. Godel's proof depends on constructing a metalanguage within the object-language itself — a formal system that can encode statements about its own operations. This self-referential metalanguage generates both the power (expressiveness, self-awareness) and the limitation (incompleteness, undecidability) of sufficiently complex formal systems. The parallel to Barthes is suggestive: myth, too, is a metalanguage that generates both power (ideological effectiveness) and vulnerability (susceptibility to demystification).

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

Metalanguage is a structural prerequisite for [[Interdimensional Semiotics]] itself. IS is, by definition, a metalanguage — a semiotic system that takes other semiotic systems (across dimensions) as its object of study. The question is whether the capacity for metalanguage is a universal feature of sufficiently complex sign systems or a contingent product of human cognition. The reading list suggests it may be structural: any semiotic system complex enough to develop reflexive operations — the ability to refer to its own signs — would develop metalanguage, and with it the capacity for both critical self-analysis and mythological self-deception. This connects to the IS interest in [[connectomes]] and [[The Noosphere]]: a noospheric network dense enough to sustain self-referential loops would necessarily generate metalinguistic operations. The Hofstadterian insight that self-reference produces both power and incompleteness suggests that any interdimensional semiotic system capable of metalanguage would also be necessarily incomplete — unable to fully formalize its own operations. This is not a limitation but a generative condition, the engine of narrative drift and semiotic evolution across dimensions.
