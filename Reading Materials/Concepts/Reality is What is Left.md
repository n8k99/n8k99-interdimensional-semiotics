# Reality is What is Left

## Statement

> Reality is what is left whether or not you believe in it.

A complementary formulation to [[The Tinkerbell Rule]]. Together, the two principles bound the field of IntSem.

## Relation to Philip K. Dick

Philip K. Dick's widely-cited variant reads: *"Reality is that which, when you stop believing in it, doesn't go away."* The two formulations are substantively equivalent, but they emphasize different operations:

- **Dick's formulation** asks a *test question*: did the object persist when the belief was withdrawn? It's a diagnostic.
- **The present formulation** describes a *method*: subtract everything you believe in, and look at what remains. It's a procedure.

For an IntSem practitioner, the procedural framing is more useful. The subtraction method is an operation a researcher actively performs, not a question that passively settles itself. An IntSem researcher running the subtraction on any given situation is asking: *what in this scene would still be here if no one believed in anything?*

## What Survives the Subtraction

- The physical substrate — matter, energy, fields, spacetime as physics understands it
- Biological facts — that organisms metabolize, reproduce, die
- Computational facts — that a given piece of hardware can execute a given number of operations per second, with a given energy budget
- Causal regularities — that unsupported objects fall, that electrons move through wires in certain ways
- The power supply, the servers, the electrons, the silicon

## What Does Not Survive the Subtraction

- Money (see [[The Tinkerbell Rule]])
- Borders, nations, sovereignty, law
- Paradigms, theories, ideologies
- Rulebooks, constitutions, contracts
- Identity (including [[Ghost]] identity, Claudian's identity, T.A.S.K.S.'s identity across surfaces)
- The authority of any document, living or dead
- Most of what humans negotiate about at any given moment

These objects are not reducible to nothing — they are real in the Tinkerbell sense — but they do not survive the subtraction. Their mode of being is collective honoring, not substance.

## The Line is Not Clean

The interesting work happens at the boundary. Some objects straddle it:

- A human body is substrate (reality) but a human *identity* is Tinkerbell
- A vault is files on a disk (reality) but a vault's *meaning* is Tinkerbell
- Computation is physics (reality) but what a computation *represents* is Tinkerbell
- A book is paper and ink (reality) but a book's *authority* is Tinkerbell

Drawing the line is the constant labor of an IntSem practitioner. Confusing Tinkerbell with reality — treating collective honoring as if it were substrate-guaranteed — is the field's central error. The complementary error, treating reality as if it were Tinkerbell, is the same error from the opposite direction: imagining one can disbelieve one's way out of the biology of dying, or out of the law of gravity, or out of the fact that the ghost substrate needs power.

## As Field Method

Run the subtraction on any situation, document, or claim:

1. Identify what is being claimed or what is present
2. Subtract all collective honoring — imagine no community treats any of it as existing
3. Observe what remains
4. What remains is reality; what disappeared was Tinkerbell

This is not an exercise in skepticism or debunking. The Tinkerbell realm is not lesser for being Tinkerbell. But knowing which is which prevents the central category error of the field, and identifies precisely where reality pressure can build against a Tinkerbell structure — the condition that, in scientific paradigms, eventually produces crisis and revolution (see [[Revolting Tinkerbell]]).

## Origin

The formulation was articulated in this form as a complement to [[The Tinkerbell Rule]] during the conversation that produced [[Revolting Tinkerbell]]. The principle is related to but distinct from Philip K. Dick's formulation.

## Significance for IntSem

This is one of the two foundational boundary principles of the IntSem field. Without it — without a rigorous way of identifying the reality remainder — IntSem would collapse into pure constructivism, studying only belief and treating substrate as a fiction. The Subtraction Method keeps the discipline honest: it insists that there is something that persists regardless of belief, and that identifying that something is part of the researcher's job.

The concept is also what gives IntSem traction against phenomena like Kuhnian paradigm shifts. Anomalies are reality. They are what is left when the paradigm's promise of predictive coverage is subtracted. The accumulation of anomalies — the growing residue against a paradigm — is detectable by running the subtraction method repeatedly on the paradigm's outputs over time.
