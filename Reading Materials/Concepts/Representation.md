# Representation

Representation is the process by which signs, symbols, or models stand for things — the foundational operation connecting semiotics, epistemology, and cognitive science. In its broadest sense, it names the relation between a sign and what the sign makes present to thought. [[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics|Saussure]]'s [[Sign]] is the basic unit of linguistic representation: the union of [[Signifier]] and [[Signified]] that makes a concept available to consciousness through a material form. But Saussure carefully limits representation to the internal relation within the sign, refusing to address how signs represent the external world — a limitation that subsequent theorists both exploit and contest.

[[Foucault - Order of Things|Foucault]] makes representation the organizing principle of an entire [[Episteme]]. In *The Order of Things*, the Classical age (roughly the 17th and 18th centuries) is defined by its commitment to the project of transparent representation: the conviction that the world can be exhaustively ordered through tables, taxonomies, and systems of classification. Language, in this episteme, is ideally a clear window onto being — a representational instrument whose own materiality should be invisible. [[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By|Lakoff and Johnson]] argue that representation is never neutral but always structured by embodied metaphor. We do not represent the world as it is but as our bodily experience and conceptual metaphors allow us to frame it. The metaphor UNDERSTANDING IS SEEING structures our very notion of what representation means — we "see" connections, "illuminate" problems, seek "clarity" — embedding a particular sensory modality into the epistemological concept itself.

## Cross-Book Development

[[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics|Saussure]]'s treatment of the sign in the *Course* (Part I, Chapter 1) establishes representation as an internal linguistic relation. The sign represents not a thing in the world but a concept — the signified — through a sound-image — the signifier. This internalist move is strategic: it allows Saussure to study language as a self-contained system of differential values, without reference to external reality. But it also creates the gap that every subsequent theorist on the reading list must address: what is the relationship between linguistic representation and the world? [[Foucault - Order of Things|Foucault]] historicizes this question in chapters 3-6 of *The Order of Things*. In the Renaissance episteme, representation was unnecessary because words and things were woven together by resemblance — the sign did not represent the world but participated in it. The Classical episteme broke this bond and instituted representation as the fundamental cognitive operation: general grammar represented thought, natural history represented nature, the analysis of wealth represented exchange. The twin instruments of representation were *mathesis* (the universal science of order, modeled on mathematics) and *taxinomia* (systematic classification into ordered tables). The Modern episteme (chapters 7-10) discovers that representation cannot ground itself — that language, life, and labor have their own opaque histories that resist transparent representation.

[[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]] engages representation through the lens of [[Ideology]]. Myth is a system of ideological representation — it represents the world as the bourgeoisie wishes it to appear, substituting motivated constructions for neutral descriptions. The "Great Family of Man" exhibition represents humanity as naturally united, when in fact the unity it depicts is a specific ideological construction. [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions|Kuhn]] shows that scientific representation is paradigm-dependent: the same data can be represented differently within different paradigms, and the shift from one representational framework to another constitutes a revolution. [[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By|Lakoff and Johnson]] push the critique of representation deepest, arguing in chapters 25-28 that the entire objectivist tradition — the myth that representation can be a neutral mirroring of reality — is itself a conceptual metaphor (THE MIND IS A MIRROR OF NATURE) masquerading as literal truth. Representation, in their framework, is always already metaphorical, always already embodied, always already perspectival.

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

Representation is the operation that [[Interdimensional Semiotics]] must simultaneously rely on and interrogate. IS claims to study how semiotic structures persist across dimensions — but this claim is itself a representational claim, presupposing that interdimensional semiotic phenomena can be adequately represented within a particular theoretical framework. The Foucauldian lesson is that every representational framework has its own conditions of possibility and its own blind spots. IS must therefore be reflexive about its own representational assumptions: the conceptual metaphors it uses to frame interdimensional phenomena (semiotic "mass," dimensional "translation," narrative "drift") are themselves representations that structure what can be known. The deeper IS question is whether representation is substrate-dependent or substrate-independent. If the Classical episteme's project of transparent representation failed within a single dimension — as Foucault argues it did — what hope does IS have for representing phenomena across dimensions? The answer may lie in the shift from representational to structural thinking: IS need not represent interdimensional phenomena transparently but can map their relational structures, tracking how differential values ([[Langue]]) transform across substrates. This aligns with the broader IS interest in [[connectomes]] as structural invariants that persist without requiring transparent representation.

## In the IS Reading List

- [[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics]] — Part I, Ch. 1: the sign as the basic mechanism of linguistic representation; the internalist move
- [[Foucault - Order of Things]] — Chs. 3-6: the Classical episteme organized by representation; mathesis and taxinomia as instruments of order; Chs. 7-10: the collapse of transparent representation in the Modern episteme
- [[Barthes - Mythologies]] — "Myth Today": myth as ideological representation; the bourgeois world represented as natural
- [[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By]] — Chs. 25-28: the critique of objectivist representation; metaphor as the condition of all representation
- [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions]] — Chs. 9-10: paradigm-dependent representation; incommensurability as the failure of cross-paradigm representation
