# Incommensurability

In its original formulation, incommensurability names the condition in which competing theoretical frameworks lack a common measure for direct comparison because they define key terms, legitimate problems, and standards of evidence differently. [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions|Kuhn]] introduced the concept in the context of [[Scientific Revolution|scientific revolutions]] to explain why pre- and post-revolutionary scientists talk past each other: they are not merely disagreeing about facts but operating within frameworks that assign different meanings to the same words. The concept draws from its mathematical origin — two magnitudes are incommensurable when no common unit can measure both — and applies it to the history of ideas.

[[Feyerabend - Against Method|Feyerabend]] arrived at the thesis independently, developing it along more radical lines. Where Kuhn treats incommensurability as a consequence of paradigm shifts — something that occurs at historical junctures — Feyerabend argues that rival theories are always potentially incommensurable, and that this is a reason to proliferate them rather than avoid them. The overlap between their positions is significant (pp. 150, 165, 190 of *Against Method*), but the normative conclusions diverge sharply. [[Lakoff - Women Fire and Dangerous Things|Lakoff]]'s work on categorization provides a cognitive-scientific grounding for incommensurability: if different conceptual systems structure perception itself through different idealized cognitive models (ICMs), then the inability to translate between frameworks is not a deficiency of language but a consequence of embodied cognition.

The concept thus operates across three registers: historical (Kuhn), methodological (Feyerabend), and cognitive (Lakoff). Each suggests that the barriers to cross-framework communication are deeper than vocabulary — they reach into the structure of perception and meaning-making itself.

## Cross-Book Development

Kuhn develops incommensurability most fully in the 1970 Postscript to *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (Section 5), where he identifies three components of paradigm incommensurability. First, proponents of competing paradigms disagree about the list of problems any candidate paradigm must solve. Second, within the new paradigm, old terms and concepts acquire new relationships to one another: "mass" in Newtonian mechanics is not the same concept as "mass" in Einsteinian mechanics, even though the word is identical. Third, practitioners of different paradigms practice in different worlds — they see different things when looking at the same apparatus. This third claim is the most radical and connects directly to the theory-ladenness of observation.

Feyerabend's treatment in *Against Method* strips incommensurability of Kuhn's implicit regret. For Kuhn, incommensurability is a problem — it means paradigm choices cannot be fully rational. For Feyerabend, it is a feature: incommensurability demonstrates that no single framework can capture all the facts, which is precisely why [[Proliferation]] is necessary. His discussion (pp. 150, 165, 190) emphasizes that incommensurable theories are not merely different descriptions of the same reality but constitute different realities, each with its own observational base. This connects to his account of [[Natural Interpretation|natural interpretations]]: the "observations" that seem to refute a new theory are themselves products of the old theory's interpretive framework.

Lakoff's *Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things* provides the mechanism that Kuhn and Feyerabend gesture toward but never fully articulate. His account of idealized cognitive models shows that categorization is not a neutral cognitive operation but a structured imposition of conceptual order on experience. Different ICMs produce genuinely different perceptual worlds — not as a metaphor but as a consequence of how embodied cognition works. This grounds incommensurability in cognitive architecture rather than intellectual history, suggesting that it is not a contingent feature of scientific development but a structural feature of human (and potentially non-human) meaning-making.

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

Incommensurability is arguably the central challenge of [[Interdimensional Semiotics]]. If frameworks within a single dimension — sharing the same physical substrate, the same cognitive architecture, the same evolutionary history — can be incommensurable, then the prospects for semiotic translation across dimensional boundaries appear daunting. The IS framework must determine whether there exists any level of semiotic structure that remains invariant across incommensurable systems: a semiotic deep grammar that persists beneath the surface variation of signs, or a substrate-independent principle of meaning that survives translation even when no common measure exists.

The concept bears directly on [[The Noosphere]] and the question of [[connectomes]]: if different cognitive architectures produce incommensurable frameworks, then noospheric structures that depend on shared meaning would be localized to regions of commensurability. The boundaries of these regions — the zones where incommensurability begins — would constitute the IS equivalent of event horizons: points beyond which semiotic signals cannot be decoded by the receiving system. Understanding whether these horizons are absolute or merely practical is one of the foundational questions of interdimensional semiotics.
