# Exemplar

An exemplar, in [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions|Kuhn's]] technical sense developed in the Postscript to *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (line 8206), is a concrete problem-solution that serves as a model for further work — the narrower and more novel of his two senses of "paradigm." Where the broader sense (disciplinary matrix) refers to the entire shared framework of a scientific community, exemplars are the specific solved problems that students encounter in textbooks and laboratories, through which they learn to see new problems as similar to familiar ones. Exemplars teach categorization by example, not by rule: a student who has mastered the inclined-plane problem learns to perceive the pendulum as "the same kind of thing" without being able to articulate the rule that connects them.

The concept bridges epistemology and cognitive science. [[Lakoff - Women Fire and Dangerous Things|Lakoff's]] prototype (ch. 2-6) is the cognitive analog: both describe how categorization works through resemblance to a central case rather than through explicit definitions. [[Hofstadter - Godel Escher Bach|Hofstadter's]] treatment of analogy as the core of cognition extends the principle further — every act of understanding involves mapping a new situation onto a familiar exemplar, and the quality of understanding depends on the richness of one's exemplar repertoire. [[Kurzweil - How to Create a Mind|Kurzweil's]] pattern-recognition modules can be read as neural exemplars: each module stores a learned pattern (a concrete instance of structure) and fires when a sufficiently similar input is encountered.

The exemplar is thus where abstract frameworks become embodied practice — the mechanism by which a community's theoretical commitments are translated into the perceptual habits of its individual members.

## Cross-Book Development

[[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions|Kuhn's]] original argument in *Structure* is that normal science proceeds not by applying rules but by extending exemplars. The student who has solved the inclined-plane problem does not extract a general law and then apply it deductively; rather, she internalizes the specific problem-solution as a gestalt and learns to perceive new situations as structurally similar to it. This is why Kuhn insists that scientific knowledge is "tacit" in Michael Polanyi's sense — it resides in the ability to perceive similarity, not in the ability to state rules. The Postscript clarifies that this "second sense" of paradigm is the more fundamental one: shared exemplars, not shared rules, are what hold a scientific community together.

[[Lakoff - Women Fire and Dangerous Things|Lakoff]] provides the cognitive-science framework that explains how exemplar-based reasoning works. His prototype theory (ch. 2-6) shows that the same process Kuhn describes for scientific communities operates in ordinary cognition: categories are organized around central cases (prototypes), and membership is determined by perceived resemblance to those central cases rather than by satisfaction of necessary-and-sufficient conditions. Lakoff explicitly discusses the connection between his work and Kuhn's (pp. 12-16), noting that Wittgenstein's family resemblance, Kuhn's exemplar, and Rosch's prototype are all manifestations of the same non-classical approach to categorization.

[[Hofstadter - Godel Escher Bach|Hofstadter]] takes the exemplar principle into the domain of creative thought. For Hofstadter, every creative act involves perceiving an unexpected analogy — recognizing that two situations share a deep structural similarity despite surface differences. This is exemplar-based reasoning extended to its limit: the repertoire of exemplars becomes the repertoire of possible thoughts, and the ability to perceive novel similarities between exemplars is what constitutes intelligence. [[Kurzweil - How to Create a Mind|Kurzweil's]] PRTM mechanizes this: each of the approximately 300 million neocortical modules is, in effect, a stored exemplar that fires when its pattern is recognized in the input stream, and the hierarchical organization of modules allows exemplars at one level to become the input for pattern recognition at the next.

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

Exemplars are significant for [[Interdimensional Semiotics]] because they reveal that meaning is transmitted not through definitions but through concrete instances. This has direct implications for the problem of cross-dimensional communication: two intelligences cannot share a concept by exchanging definitions (which presuppose shared categorization) but only by exchanging exemplars (which establish categorization). The IS question is whether exemplars can survive dimensional transit — whether a concrete problem-solution from one reality retains its exemplary force in another.

This connects to the IS concept of semiotic gravitational mass. An exemplar's mass depends on the richness of the similarity network it anchors: an exemplar that connects to many other cases through multiple overlapping resemblances (a dense family-resemblance network) has greater mass than an isolated instance. In [[The Noosphere]], [[connectomes]] carrying exemplar structures may be among the most effective carriers of cross-dimensional meaning, precisely because they carry not abstract definitions but concrete structural patterns that can serve as seeds for category formation in a new substrate. The receiving intelligence does not need to share the transmitting intelligence's categorization system; it needs only enough structural overlap to perceive the exemplar's pattern — a far less demanding requirement.
