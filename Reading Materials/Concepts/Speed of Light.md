# c Speed of Light

## Definition

The **speed of light in vacuum**, denoted $c$, is a universal physical constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= 299\,792\,458\text{ m/s}\approx 3.00\times 10^8\text{ m/s} \\[6pt]
c &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\,\mu_0}} \quad(\varepsilon_0,\mu_0 \text{ are the vacuum permittivity and permeability}) \\[6pt]
\gamma &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} \quad\text{(Lorentz factor)} \\[6pt]
E &= m\,c^2 \quad\text{(mass–energy equivalence)}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Roles in Physics

### A Universal Speed Limit

No matter how you are moving, you measure light in vacuum travelling at exactly $c$. Nothing with rest mass can be accelerated all the way to $c$ without infinite energy — so $c$ is a hard ceiling on the propagation of causal signals.

### The Glue Between Space and Time

Special relativity shows that to keep $c$ invariant for every observer, distances and time intervals must stretch and contract (time dilation, length contraction). This reshuffling of space and time into a unified **spacetime** is the geometric consequence of $c$'s invariance.

### Born from Electromagnetism

Maxwell's equations imply that changing electric and magnetic fields propagate as waves at exactly $c$ — so light, radio, X-rays, gamma rays, and every electromagnetic phenomenon share this same propagation speed. Historically, it was the match between the empirically measured speed of light and the $c$ predicted by Maxwell that first revealed light *is* an electromagnetic wave.

### Mass–Energy Equivalence

Einstein's $E = m c^2$ states that mass itself is a form of stored energy, with $c^2$ as the conversion factor. The enormous value of $c^2$ is why a small amount of mass corresponds to a vast amount of energy.

### A Cosmic Ruler

Because light takes time to travel, looking out into space is also looking back in time. The observable universe's size, cosmological horizons, and the synchronisation of clocks across the globe (GPS, time standards) all depend on the finite, invariant value of $c$.

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

$c$ is the paradigmatic physical [[Topological Invariant|invariant]] — the thing that stays the same for every observer, in every frame, across every continuous transformation of the coordinate system. It is the prototype for what IS calls an invariant under [[Monodromy|monodromy]]: a property of the substrate that is preserved no matter which path through the manifold the observer takes. In any attempt to formalise what it means for *meaning* to persist across translations, the invariance of $c$ is the reference picture — a single, sharp, dimensionally unambiguous quantity whose constancy is the defining feature of the physical substrate it lives on.

When [[HEMM Space]] crosses [[Sheet|sheets]] with different base physics, $c$ itself may differ. What persists across the crossing are the *relationships* between $c$ and other constants — dimensionless ratios like the fine-structure constant $\alpha = e^2 / (4\pi \varepsilon_0 \hbar c)$. The observation that $c$ is sheet-variable but ratios of $c$ with other fundamental constants are sheet-invariant is the IS-grade lesson: **the true invariants are rarely the numbers you first notice; they are the ratios that make the numbers meaningful.**
