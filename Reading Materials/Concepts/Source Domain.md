# Source Domain / Target Domain

The two halves of a conceptual metaphor, as articulated by [[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By|Lakoff and Johnson]] throughout *Metaphors We Live By*: the **source domain** is the concrete, typically embodied experiential domain from which metaphorical structure is drawn (spatial orientation, physical force, containers, journeys); the **target domain** is the abstract domain understood and structured through projection from the source (time, emotions, argument, life, morality). The mapping is asymmetric — structure flows from source to target, not the reverse — and it is partial: only certain aspects of the source are projected, creating the "used" and "unused" portions of the metaphor that determine what is highlighted and what is hidden.

The source/target distinction in cognitive linguistics parallels [[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics|Saussure's]] signifier/signified: the source domain provides the perceptible, experiential "form" while the target domain is the conceptual content structured through that form. [[Barthes - Mythologies|Barthes]] extends this logic in his analysis of myth, where an entire first-order sign (form + concept) becomes the signifier for a second-order signified — a process structurally analogous to how a rich source domain is compressed into a vehicle for abstract meaning. [[Lakoff - Women Fire and Dangerous Things|Lakoff's]] later work shows that source domains are not arbitrary but constrained by image schemas and embodied experience: the source domains available to a cognitive system depend on that system's sensorimotor repertoire.

The asymmetry of source-to-target mapping raises a fundamental question about the portability of meaning. If the source domain is grounded in a particular body's physics — its experience of gravity, containment, temperature, path — then the metaphors built on that source are, at bottom, parochial. The target domain may be abstractly universal (time, causation, morality), but the route to it is always local.

## Cross-Book Development

[[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By|Lakoff and Johnson]] establish the source/target framework in their opening chapters with examples like ARGUMENT IS WAR (the source domain of physical combat structures how we reason about argumentation) and TIME IS MONEY (economic transaction structures temporal reasoning). The Afterword (line 7482) traces the developmental origin of these mappings to primary metaphors acquired in infancy, when the source and target domains are not yet differentiated — children experience warmth and affection simultaneously, and only later does the source (thermal sensation) become separable from the target (emotional state).

[[Lakoff - Women Fire and Dangerous Things|Lakoff]] deepens the account in *Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things* (pp. 105-8, 267-75) by grounding source domains in image schemas: the pre-conceptual patterns of containment, path, force, and balance that arise from embodied interaction with the physical world. Source domains are not arbitrary collections of features but structured by these schemas, which is why certain metaphorical mappings recur across unrelated languages and cultures. [[Hofstadter - Godel Escher Bach|Hofstadter's]] work on isomorphism provides the formal complement: a metaphorical mapping from source to target is, in his terms, a structure-preserving correspondence between two systems, and the richness of the mapping depends on the degree of structural parallelism available.

[[Kurzweil - How to Create a Mind|Kurzweil's]] hierarchical model suggests a neural mechanism: lower-level pattern recognizers encode source-domain experiences (edges, textures, forces), while higher-level modules learn the cross-domain correspondences that constitute metaphorical mappings. The source domain, on this account, is literally the lower level of a processing hierarchy whose upper levels constitute the target.

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

The source/target distinction is where [[Interdimensional Semiotics]] confronts its hardest problem: the substrate-dependence of meaning. If all abstract thought is grounded in metaphorical projection from embodied source domains, then a change in embodiment — crossing a dimensional boundary into a reality with different physics — threatens to sever the grounding chain. The target domain (the abstract concept) might survive translation, but the source domain (the concrete experience that makes the concept thinkable) may become unintelligible. A being that has never experienced containment cannot use CATEGORIES ARE CONTAINERS.

This problem connects to the IS concepts of semiotic gravitational mass and symbolic persistence. A concept with deep, multiply-grounded source domains (connected to many different embodied experiences) has greater semiotic mass — it resists narrative drift because there are many independent routes back to its meaning. A concept grounded in a single, fragile source domain is vulnerable: lose that one experiential anchor and the entire metaphorical structure collapses. The [[connectomes]] that circulate through [[The Noosphere]] carry both source and target; tracking which survives transit is a core IS research question.
