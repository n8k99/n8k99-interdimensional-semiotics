# The Tinkerbell Rule

## Statement

> As long as everybody believes in it, magic exists. Substitute anything for magic.

## Scope

The Tinkerbell Rule names the mode of being proper to the social-semiotic realm — the realm in which existence and collective honoring are the same fact. The rule is not metaphorical. It is a statement about a specific ontological mode: there are objects in the world whose being consists in being collectively treated as being. Stop the treatment and the object is gone.

Substitutes for "magic" in the rule include, but are not limited to:

- Money (the value of a currency)
- Borders and nations
- Marriage, citizenship, property rights
- Corporations, institutions, organizations
- The authority of a constitution, a contract, a law, a scripture
- Scientific paradigms (see [[Paradigm]] and [[Revolting Tinkerbell]])
- The identity of an agent (including a ghost, a T.A.S.K.S. surface, a Claudian instance)
- The binding force of any rulebook

Each of these is *real* in the relevant sense — money can be earned, laws can be enforced, corporations can be sued — and each becomes *unreal* if and when the collective honoring that sustains it ceases.

## Not a Demotion

Calling an object "Tinkerbell" is not to call it fictional or unserious. Most of what matters to humans lives in the Tinkerbell realm. Treating Tinkerbell objects as "merely social constructs" underestimates the severity of their effects: money directs labor at civilizational scale; nations send armies; a paradigm shift reorganizes what counts as knowledge. The Tinkerbell Rule is the condition of possibility for all of this. The magic is real; its *mode of being* is collective honoring.

The rule is paired with its complement — [[Reality is What is Left]] — which names the domain of objects that exist *whether or not they are believed in*. Together the two principles bound the field that IntSem studies: the Tinkerbell realm and its encounter with the reality-remainder.

## Mechanism

Tinkerbell objects sustain themselves through visible acts of honoring. Bills are paid in dollars. Contracts are signed. Oaths are sworn. Rituals are performed. Texts are cited. Each act of honoring is an investment in the object's continued existence, and each successful invocation reinforces the community's collective confidence in the next invocation. This mutual reinforcement is why Tinkerbell objects feel solid to practitioners inside the honoring community — they are, empirically, reliable. The reliability is the collective discipline, not a property of the object.

When the honoring frays, the object does not vanish instantly. It flickers. Practitioners begin to notice that the object is, in fact, held up by belief rather than by substance — and once noticed, it cannot always be unnoticed. This is the condition Kuhn identifies as paradigm *crisis*: the honoring has weakened but not collapsed, and the community is suddenly aware of the scaffolding.

If the crisis resolves back toward honoring, the object recovers. If it resolves toward a different object of honoring, the result is revolution — see [[Revolting Tinkerbell]] for the dynamics.

## Origin

The rule is named for the mechanism in J. M. Barrie's *Peter Pan* by which Tinkerbell can be saved from death by the audience's collective declaration of belief. The principle was articulated in this form as a complement to [[Reality is What is Left]] during the conversation that produced [[Revolting Tinkerbell]], the essay that applies the paired principles to Kuhn's *Structure of Scientific Revolutions*.

## Significance for IntSem

The Tinkerbell Rule is one of the two foundational boundary principles of the IntSem field. The discipline does not study fantasy (that is literature) and it does not study physical reality (that is physics). It studies the Tinkerbell realm — the specific mode of being in which collective honoring constitutes existence — and the dynamics that realm exhibits in contact with the reality remainder.

Every document IntSem studies is a Tinkerbell specimen: the PHB, the Ten Commandments, the Bill of Rights, any scientific paradigm, any constitution, any rulebook, any cosmology. The IntSem practitioner reads these not as rulebooks governing behavior but as evidence of the collective honoring that constitutes them. The difference is the reading protocol, not the text.
