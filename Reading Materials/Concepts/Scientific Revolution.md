# Scientific Revolution

In [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions|Kuhn]]'s account, a scientific revolution is a non-cumulative episode in the history of science in which an older [[Paradigm]] is replaced entirely by a new, incompatible one. Elaborated in Chapter IX of *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, the concept overturns the textbook image of science as steady accumulation: revolutions are wholesale transformations of the conceptual framework, after which practitioners literally perceive a different world. The process begins with accumulated [[Anomaly|anomalies]] that resist resolution under [[Normal Science]], escalates through crisis (Chapters VII-VIII), and culminates in a gestalt switch that is irreversible — one cannot unsee what the new paradigm reveals.

[[Feyerabend - Against Method|Feyerabend]] draws on the historical record of scientific revolutions to support his methodological anarchism: every fixed rule of scientific method has been productively violated during at least one major revolution. Galileo's defense of Copernicanism, which Feyerabend examines in detail (chapters 6-13), succeeded not by following established methodology but by replacing [[Natural Interpretation|natural interpretations]], deploying propaganda, and proceeding counterinductively against the best evidence of his time. [[Baudrillard - Simulacra and Simulation|Baudrillard]] offers the postmodern counterpart to revolution: [[Implosion]], in which categories do not shift to a new configuration but collapse inward, eliminating the distinctions (real/imaginary, meaning/media) that made the old framework coherent.

The concept is thus triangulated across three positions: Kuhn's structured transition (crisis, revolution, new normal science), Feyerabend's anarchic rupture (no fixed pattern, anything goes), and Baudrillard's terminal collapse (no new paradigm emerges — only the implosion of the conditions that made paradigms possible).

## Cross-Book Development

Kuhn's treatment of scientific revolutions in *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (Chapter IX, "The Nature and Necessity of Scientific Revolutions") argues that revolutions are structurally analogous to political revolutions: they involve a fundamental reconstitution of the community's governing framework, and they cannot be resolved by appeal to the standards of the old regime. The key mechanism is [[Incommensurability]] (elaborated in the Postscript, Section 5): pre- and post-revolutionary paradigms define terms, problems, and standards differently enough that neutral comparison is impossible. Scientists on either side of a revolution are, in a meaningful sense, working in different worlds.

Feyerabend radicalizes this insight in *Against Method*. Where Kuhn treats revolution as an exceptional but structured event — preceded by crisis, driven by anomaly accumulation, resulting in a new paradigm — Feyerabend argues that the revolutionary stance should be permanent. His principle of [[Counterinduction]] is essentially revolution as method: the deliberate, ongoing introduction of hypotheses that contradict established results. The case study of Galileo (chapters 6-13) demonstrates that successful revolution requires not just new evidence but new standards of evidence — a wholesale replacement of the interpretive framework through which evidence is evaluated. Feyerabend's famous "anything goes" is not a positive principle but what remains after every methodological rule has been shown to have been violated during some successful revolution.

Baudrillard's *Simulacra and Simulation* recasts the question entirely. In Baudrillard's framework, the era of revolutions — understood as transitions from one representational regime to another — is over. The third order of simulacra (the simulation era) eliminates the referential ground that made revolution meaningful: when the real has been replaced by its model, there is nothing left to revolt against. What Kuhn called "crisis" becomes what Baudrillard calls "implosion" — not the replacement of one framework by another but the collapse of the very distinction between framework and reality. This marks a boundary condition for the concept of scientific revolution: it may be historically specific to an era in which the difference between model and reality was still operative.

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

Scientific revolution is central to [[Interdimensional Semiotics]] because it provides the most developed model of what happens when one semiotic framework is replaced by another. The IS question of symbolic persistence across dimensions is, at root, a question about whether signs can survive revolutionary transitions — not merely within a single intellectual tradition, but across radically different substrates of meaning-making. Kuhn's observation that post-revolutionary scientists inhabit a different perceptual world suggests that dimensional boundaries may function as permanent revolution: a transition so total that no sign retains its pre-crossing meaning.

The concept also bears on narrative drift within [[The Noosphere]]. If semiotic structures undergo revolutionary discontinuities, then the persistence of meaning across dimensions cannot be modeled as gradual drift but must account for catastrophic phase transitions — moments where the entire sign-system reconfigures. The IS framework must determine whether such transitions preserve any invariant semiotic content, or whether [[Incommensurability]] is the inevitable condition of cross-dimensional contact.
