# Paradigm

In [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions|Kuhn]]'s original formulation, a paradigm is the entire constellation of beliefs, values, techniques, and exemplary achievements shared by a scientific community. It is not merely a theory or a model but the total framework that defines what counts as a legitimate problem, an acceptable method, and a satisfactory solution. In the 1970 Postscript, Kuhn refined the concept into two components: the "disciplinary matrix" (the broader set of shared commitments, symbolic generalizations, and values) and the "exemplar" (concrete problem-solutions that students learn by imitation and that guide future research).

[[Feyerabend - Against Method|Feyerabend]] engages directly with Kuhn's paradigm concept but reverses its normative valence. Where Kuhn treats single-paradigm dominance as a necessary condition for productive science, Feyerabend sees it as intellectual monopoly that suppresses alternatives and blinds practitioners to evidence available only from rival perspectives. [[Baudrillard - Simulacra and Simulation|Baudrillard]] extends the paradigm's reality-shaping power into a more radical register: if a paradigm determines what its practitioners can perceive, then it functions as a simulation engine, generating a "real" that has no existence independent of the model. [[Lakoff - Women Fire and Dangerous Things|Lakoff]]'s work on idealized cognitive models similarly suggests that paradigms are not merely professional agreements but cognitive architectures that structure perception at a pre-conscious level.

The paradigm concept thus sits at the intersection of epistemology, sociology of knowledge, and cognitive science. It raises the question that motivates much of the IS reading list: to what extent does the framework through which we interpret signs determine the reality those signs can represent?

## Cross-Book Development

Kuhn introduced the paradigm concept in *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) as the organizing principle of scientific communities, elaborating it across Chapters II-IV through the mechanics of [[Normal Science]]. The concept's initial breadth proved both its strength and its vulnerability: critics counted over twenty distinct uses of "paradigm" in the first edition, prompting Kuhn's Postscript refinement into disciplinary matrix and exemplar. The disciplinary matrix captures the sociological dimension — shared commitments, values, and symbolic generalizations — while the exemplar captures the pedagogical one: the concrete problem-solutions through which students internalize a paradigm's tacit knowledge.

Feyerabend's engagement with the paradigm in *Against Method* (chapters 2-3) transforms it from a descriptive category into a polemical target. His principle of [[Proliferation]] is explicitly anti-paradigmatic: rather than working within a single framework, scientists should multiply rival theories precisely because paradigmatic consensus suppresses the kind of evidence that only alternative frameworks can reveal. The consistency condition — the demand that new hypotheses agree with accepted theories — is, for Feyerabend, nothing more than the paradigm's immune system protecting itself from [[Counterinduction]]. Where Kuhn sees productive constraint, Feyerabend sees epistemic censorship.

Baudrillard's *Simulacra and Simulation* never uses the term "paradigm," but his account of how models generate reality without origin (the precession of simulacra) can be read as the paradigm concept taken to its logical extreme. If Kuhn showed that paradigms determine what scientists can observe, Baudrillard asks what happens when the model no longer merely shapes perception but replaces reality altogether. Lakoff's cognitive linguistics provides the mechanism: idealized cognitive models (ICMs) structure categorization at the level of embodied cognition, suggesting that paradigm-dependence is not a feature of professional science alone but of human meaning-making as such.

## Significance for Interdimensional Semiotics

The paradigm is the foundational concept for [[Interdimensional Semiotics]] because it establishes that meaning is always framework-dependent. If signs acquire their significance only within a paradigm's disciplinary matrix, then contact between dimensions — each presumably operating under radically different semiotic paradigms — raises the question of whether any sign can maintain semantic stability across such boundaries. The concept of semiotic gravitational mass depends on understanding what gives a sign weight within a given paradigm: its exemplar status, its integration into the disciplinary matrix, its role in sustaining [[Normal Science]].

The deeper IS question is whether paradigms are substrate-dependent. Kuhn assumed a shared physical world that different paradigms interpret differently; [[The Noosphere]] and [[connectomes]] suggest that the substrate of meaning itself may vary. If paradigms are cognitive architectures (as Lakoff implies), then dimensional variation in cognitive substrate would produce not merely different interpretations of the same reality but genuinely incommensurable modes of semiosis — paradigms without a shared world to disagree about.
