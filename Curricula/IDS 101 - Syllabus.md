# IDS 101 — Interdimensional Semiotics: A Field in Construction

**Semester:** Fall, 14 weeks
**Institution:** *(Liberal Arts University, ~25 students max for Socratic function)*
**Instructor:** Eckenrode, N.
**Office Hours:** By appointment; AI-mediated written exchanges between sessions also welcome.

---

## Course Description

[[Interdimensional Semiotics - Academic Field Summary|Interdimensional Semiotics]] is a field-in-construction that sits in the venn-overlap between Computer Science and the Humanities. It studies how meaning persists, transforms, and dissolves across substrates — between paradigms, between cosmologies, between languages, between biological and artificial cognition, between collective honoring and material reality. This course is the introduction to the field's two foundational principles, its core reading list, and its operative methodology.

The course is also, structurally, **a course in personal information management and AI-mediated knowledge work**. The texts are the substrate on which the meta-skill is practiced. By the end of the semester, students should be able to engage substantive academic material with AI assistance honestly, articulate their own process, and defend both *what they argue* and *how they came to argue it*.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the semester, students will be able to:

1. Articulate the two principles of IntSem ([[The Tinkerbell Rule]], [[Reality is What is Left]]) and apply them as analytical tools to texts and to live cultural phenomena
2. Read and synthesize academic-level material across philosophy, semiotics, cognitive science, network theory, and AI research
3. Operate AI tools as substrate-engaged collaborators rather than as essay-generators — including describing their workflow honestly
4. Defend their positions and processes in real-time Socratic dialogue
5. Write essays that demonstrate the integration of multiple texts under a single analytical frame
6. Distinguish what they observe (substrate) from what is collectively honored about it (Tinkerbell)

---

## Required Materials

Students must have working access to the following texts, available via library, purchase, or fair-use scan. The full [[Interdimensional Semiotics Reading List]] is provided as a course document for reference; **only the following selections are required reading.**

The course also assumes:
- A working AI tool (Claude, ChatGPT, or equivalent)
- A note-taking system the student maintains across the semester
- Email access for essay submission

---

## Grading

The entire grade is based on two components, weighted equally:

- **Classroom participation (50%)** — quality and substance of contribution to Socratic discussion, including the ability to defend essay content and process under questioning
- **Submission compliance (50%)** — submission of every essay no later than one hour before each class meeting; lateness = 0 for that week

**There is no traditional grading of essay quality.** The essay is preparation for the discussion, not assessment in itself. The discussion is where assessment happens.

---

## Course Policies

### On AI use

**AI use is assumed and welcome.** Students are expected to use AI tools to engage the readings, draft their essays, and prepare their thinking. *Concealment of AI use is not required and is, in fact, counterproductive* — the seminar discusses *how* essays were prepared as much as *what* they argue.

### On submission

Essays must be emailed to the instructor no later than **one hour before each class meeting**. Late submissions are not graded for credit. Extensions are not granted. The deadline is the system's spine.

### On essay form

- Length: 8–10 pages (standard academic format)
- Source materials: the assigned readings for the week; outside sources welcome but not required
- Format: standard prose; citations as needed
- The essay must engage *all* assigned readings for the week, not a subset

### On classroom dialogue

Discussion is Socratic. The instructor leads questioning, but students are expected to engage each other directly as well. **The instructor uses AI to analyze submitted essays before class** and identifies which students will be questioned first based on the analysis. *The analytical view is not shared with students.* Students are responsible for being prepared to defend any aspect of what they wrote.

## Week-by-Week Schedule

### Week 1 — *What does IntSem mean to you?*

- **No prior reading.** Bring a 2–3 page personal essay on what you think Interdimensional Semiotics might be, based purely on the words *interdimensional* and *semiotics* and your own intuition.
- **In class:** open discussion. The instructor introduces the two principles. The class develops a working baseline of what each student arrived with.
- **For next week:** acquire and begin Saussure.

### Week 2 — *The Sign and the Substrate*

- **IntSem readings:**
  - [[Saussure - Course in General Linguistics]] — chapters on the [[Sign|sign]], [[Signifier|signifier]], [[Signified|signified]]
  - [[The Tinkerbell Rule]] and [[Reality is What is Left]] (vault concept notes)
- **AI paper:** Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950) — the founding question, *can machines think*, posed as a Tinkerbell-vs-Reality test
- **Essay:** What is the relationship between Saussure's sign and the IntSem two-principles framing? Where does Turing's question land within that framing?

### Week 3 — *Paradigms and Their Failures*

- **IntSem readings:**
  - [[Kuhn - Structure of Scientific Revolutions]] — selections (Chs. 1–5, 9–10)
  - [[Revolting Tinkerbell]] (essay)
- **AI paper:** Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major, Shmitchell, "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big?" (2021)
- **Essay:** How does the Stochastic Parrots paper read as a Kuhnian crisis-document? What [[Paradigm|paradigm]] does it claim to revolt against, and what new paradigm does it implicitly propose?

### Week 4 — *Discourse, Episteme, Order*

- **IntSem readings:**
  - [[Foucault - Order of Things]] — preface, Chs. 1, 9, conclusion
  - Glossary entries: [[Episteme]], [[Discourse]]
- **AI paper:** Bommasani et al., "On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models" (2021)
- **Essay:** Bommasani et al. attempt to map the entire field of foundation models. Is this a Foucauldian *episteme*-formation event? What does the paper make thinkable that wasn't before, and what does it foreclose?

### Week 5 — *Conceptual Metaphor and What We Live By*

- **IntSem readings:**
  - [[Lakoff Johnson - Metaphors We Live By]] — Chs. 1–7
- **AI paper:** Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need" (2017)
- **Essay:** The Transformer architecture relies on the metaphor of *attention*. What is this metaphor doing? What understanding does it enable, and what does it obscure? How does the metaphor's power relate to Lakoff & Johnson's account of how metaphors structure thought?

### Week 6 — *Categorization, Family Resemblance, Prototype*

- **IntSem readings:**
  - [[Lakoff - Women Fire and Dangerous Things]] — Chs. 1–4, 8
- **AI paper:** Mitchell et al., "Model Cards for Model Reporting" (2019)
- **Essay:** Model Cards attempt to categorize ML models by their characteristics and limitations. Read this attempt through Lakoff's account of [[Categorization]]. Does the Model Cards framework reify a problematic category structure, or does it accommodate the prototype effects Lakoff identifies?

### Week 7 — *Pattern Recognition as Mind*

- **IntSem readings:**
  - [[Kurzweil - How to Create a Mind]] — Chs. 1–4, 7
  - [[The Outboard Cortex]] (essay)
- **AI paper:** Olah et al., "A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits" (Anthropic, 2021)
- **Essay:** Where do Kurzweil's pattern-recognizer hierarchy and Olah's transformer circuits agree, and where do they diverge? Is the *Outboard Cortex* essay's framing of cognition supported, complicated, or refuted by the technical details of transformer circuits?

### Week 8 — *Hyperreality and the Precession of Simulacra*

- **IntSem readings:**
  - [[Baudrillard - Simulacra and Simulation]] — Chs. 1, 2, 5
- **AI paper:** Park et al., "Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior" (2023)
- **Essay:** Park et al. literally use *simulacra* in their title. Are their generative agents Baudrillardian [[Simulacrum|simulacra]], and if so, of what order? What does Baudrillard's framework predict about the trajectory of such systems?

### Week 9 — *Strange Loops, Tangled Hierarchies, Self-Reference*

- **IntSem readings:**
  - [[Hofstadter - Godel Escher Bach]] — Introduction, Chs. 1, 9, 14, 20
- **AI paper:** Hubinger et al., "Risks from Learned Optimization in Advanced Machine Learning Systems" (2019) — focus on mesa-optimization sections
- **Essay:** Mesa-optimization (an optimizer learning to contain its own optimizer) is structurally a [[Strange Loop|strange loop]] in Hofstadter's sense. Is the alignment problem this paper identifies a Hofstadterian self-reference problem, and if so, does Hofstadter's analysis offer any traction on it?

### Week 10 — *Networks, Hubs, Scale-Free Structures*

- **IntSem readings:**
  - [[Barabasi - Linked]] — Chs. 1, 5, 7, 9
- **AI paper:** Kaplan et al., "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models" (2020)
- **Essay:** Both texts study emergent structure in large systems. How do scaling laws in neural networks compare to [[Scale-free Network|scale-free network]] properties in social and biological systems? Is there a deeper isomorphism, or are these just superficial analogies?

### Week 11 — *Distributed Knowledge, Common Knowledge, Coordination*

- **IntSem readings:**
  - [[Halpern Moses - Knowledge and Common Knowledge in a Distributed Environment]] — selections
  - [[Gilbert Lynch - Brewers Conjecture and the CAP Theorem]] — selections
- **AI paper:** Andreas, "Language Models as Agent Models" (2022)
- **Essay:** Halpern & Moses prove [[Common Knowledge|common knowledge]] is impossible under unreliable communication. Andreas argues language models can be read as agent models. How do the distributed-systems impossibility results constrain or shape what language models can and cannot represent about agents and their knowledge?

### Week 12 — *Hyperspace, Topology, Multi-Dimensional Cognition*

- **IntSem readings:**
  - [[Kaku - Hyperspace]] — Chs. 1–3, 5
  - [[Penrose - Road to Reality]] — selections from Chs. 14–15 (manifolds and bundles)
- **AI paper:** Anthropic, "Towards Monosemanticity: Decomposing Language Models with Dictionary Learning" (2024) — interpretability work that treats neural representations as high-dimensional structures
- **Essay:** Both the physics texts and the interpretability paper treat their subjects as living in high-dimensional manifold spaces. Is this a productive metaphor or a literal description in each case? What does IntSem gain by adopting topological language for semiotic phenomena?

### Week 13 — *Method, Counterinduction, the Refusal of Method*

- **IntSem readings:**
  - [[Feyerabend - Against Method]] — Introduction, Chs. 1, 2, 16, 18
- **AI paper:** Anil et al., "Many-shot Jailbreaking" (2024) **OR** Christiano et al., "Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences" (2017)
- **Essay:** Feyerabend argues for methodological pluralism — *anything goes* — against any single privileged method. Is the AI safety / alignment field operating with Feyerabendian pluralism, or has it converged on an orthodoxy? What does Feyerabend's stance recommend about how to evaluate alignment proposals?

### Week 14 — *Synthesis: IntSem as a Field*

- **IntSem readings:**
  - [[Revolting Tinkerbell]] (re-read, with the semester behind you)
  - [[The Outboard Cortex]] (re-read, same)
  - [[Interdimensional Semiotics - Academic Field Summary|The IntSem Field Summary]] document
- **AI paper:** Anthropic, "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback" (2022) — already in the reading list
- **Essay:** Constitutional AI attempts to operationalize ethical principles inside an AI system through self-critique. Read this attempt as an IntSem case study: where does it operate as Tinkerbell-honoring (collective principles imposed by the developers' choices), where does it operate as Reality (substrate constraints that hold regardless), and where does it sit in the venn-overlap of ComSci and Humanities that IntSem inhabits? Use the semester's full reading to support your analysis.

---

## Final Class

The final class meeting is reserved for an open Socratic discussion on the question:

**"How has your understanding of Interdimensional Semiotics changed across the semester, and what do you now think the field is for?"**

No essay is required for this class. The discussion itself is the final assessment.

## A Note on This Course's Place in a Curriculum

IDS 101 is the introductory course of a discipline-in-construction. Subsequent courses, *should they ever be offered*, would deepen specific tracks:

- **IDS 201 — Semiotics and Information**: Saussure's full *Course*, Barthes' *Mythologies*, Eco's semiotic theory, Sebeok's biosemiotics, contemporary AI interpretability work
- **IDS 220 — Cognitive Architectures Across Substrates**: Hofstadter, Lakoff, embodied cognition, neural network architectures, the extended mind thesis
- **IDS 240 — Distributed Epistemology**: Halpern–Moses, CAP theorem, consensus protocols, multi-agent AI systems, common-knowledge dynamics
- **IDS 260 — Cosmology and Topology**: Greene, Vilenkin, Smolin, manifold structure, multi-cosmological semiotic models
- **IDS 301 — Method and Anti-Method**: Kuhn, Feyerabend, philosophy of science applied to AI research methodology
- **IDS 320 — The Hyperreal**: Baudrillard, simulacra theory, generative agents, the precession of simulacra in the AI age
- **IDS 401 — Senior Seminar**: original research project; student designs and executes a piece of IntSem analysis on a substrate of their choice

The 101 course establishes the principles, methodology, and intellectual community necessary to enter the further work. Whether further work exists for a given student depends on their development across the semester and their own interests beyond it.
